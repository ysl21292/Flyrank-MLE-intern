Ranking Pages to Review First: A Client-Held-Out Test

Jay Rasal · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane

Abstract. Which web pages should an editor check first because their search views are falling? Using 30,000 anonymized pages from 32 clients, I defined "declining" as a drop of more than 20% in impressions (last 30 days vs the 30 before) and ranked pages with a transparent rule, logistic regression and a random forest. I tested all three on clients never seen in training (5-fold, grouped by client). Both models beat random picking (0.54) at Precision@50, and logistic regression scored 0.80 against 0.74 for the rule, but the spread between folds (0.62 to 0.88) is too large to call a clear win. Much of the skill comes from 90-day traffic totals that overlap the label window, so the result is directional decision-support, not proof that refreshing pages helps.

1. Introduction

Editors have far more pages than time. The decision this work supports: which pages get a human review this month. A wrong pick wastes editor time on a healthy page, or lets a slipping page go unnoticed. The output is a ranked list with a reason code and a suggested action, and a person makes the final call.

2. Data

The FlyRank ML Internship starter release: 30,000 pages from 32 pseudonymized clients, one row per page, with trailing 90-day search and analytics metrics and a 30-day vs previous 30-day trend. The snapshot gives no calendar dates. The larger warehouse release (about 79 million daily rows) was not used for any result here. Excluded: the label sources (trend direction, trend percentage, and the last-30 and previous-30-day columns), all IDs as inputs, and any product decision flags (none are in the data). No client names, domains, URLs or queries appear in this paper.

3. Methodology

  • Label (proxy): declining = impressions in the last 30 days more than 20% below the previous 30. It is measured, but it is a short-window stand-in for lasting decline.
  • Baseline rule: a page is flagged if it is under 180 days old, untouched for 90+ days, and visible (100+ impressions, with a search position). The longer untouched, the higher it ranks. Reason code young_untouched_visible, action review_for_refresh. 1,944 pages are flagged. The thresholds came from two signal checks: staleness was mixed (decline rate 0.52 at 14 to 29 days, 0.61 at 90 to 179 days, 0.47 beyond 180 days on only 174 pages) and page age was opposite to the common belief (0.63 for pages 90 to 179 days old, 0.43 for 365+).
  • Models: logistic regression and a random forest on observed signals (traffic totals, position, click rate, page age, days since update, page type and intent). Medians fill missing values inside each training fold, with a missing flag. Seeds fixed at 42.
  • Validation: 5-fold cross-validation grouped by client, so every test client is unseen. Metrics: Precision@K and AUC, always shown next to the base rate of 0.54. Ties are broken randomly, not by file order.
  • Leakage checks: banned columns confirmed absent; a deliberately added label-derived column pushed the forest to AUC 1.00, so the test harness works; the column was then removed.

Observed: logistic regression is ahead of the rule on average, yet the rule beats it in two of five folds, so this is not a clear win. The rule flags pages in only some clients (just 7 pages in one test fold, where it was near random). The forest has the best P@20 and AUC but the least stable P@50 (0.56 to 0.90), so its extra complexity did not clearly pay off.

Permutation importance on unseen clients points to 90-day clicks (AUC drop 0.16), then impressions (0.05) and sessions (0.03). Errors: the model over-predicts decline for comparison articles (predicted 0.68, actual 0.57). Its worst misses are high-view pages with almost no clicks that held steady.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • The label is a short-window proxy, and 90-day features contain the same 60 days that define it. Results are directional, not a clean early-warning test.
  • The rule was tuned by looking at the whole file, so it had a head start. Flagged pages are concentrated: 10 clients, with the top 3 holding 94%, and about 95% share one last-update date, which looks like a batch update.
  • One fold is a single large client, and fold base rates range from 0.38 to 0.65, so single-fold numbers are noisy.
  • Observational data only. Nothing here shows that refreshing a page causes recovery, and nothing here says how Google ranks pages.

6. Ranked recommendations

  1. Use the list as a review queue, not an auto-fix. A person checks the top pages; this is decision-support.
  2. Review flagged pages in batches by client and last-update date. Many share one update date, so one cause may explain many pages.
  3. Before refreshing, rule out look-alikes: seasonality, a sibling page absorbing the traffic, and low-volume noise (under 500 views).
  4. Treat low confidence honestly: deep rankings (position 30+) and pages with no clicks are the likeliest false alarms.
  5. Monitor and retrain: rerun monthly on unseen clients; if Precision@50 falls toward the base rate, retrain or retire the rule.
  6. Next step: rebuild the label as a future window (features from the past, outcome from the next 30 days) on the warehouse release.

Do not automate: publishing edits, deleting pages, or promising traffic recovery.
